# Stablecoins as settlement rails: a CFO's guide

For a treasury team, the word "stablecoin" still trips a reflex — *isn't that crypto?* It's a fair question, and it deserves a straight answer.

The answer is that a stablecoin is not an investment. It's a **settlement instrument** — a digital dollar or euro designed to hold its value and move instantly. Used correctly, it's the closest thing finance teams have had to a programmable wire transfer.

## What a stablecoin actually is

A stablecoin is a token whose value is pegged to a fiat currency — usually the US dollar. The issuer holds reserves backing each token, and holders can redeem one token for roughly one dollar.

It's worth stating the obvious distinction: a stablecoin doesn't go up or down. It isn't a position. It's a bearer instrument for a dollar that happens to move on open, always-on rails.

## Why that matters for settlement

The features that make a stablecoin boring as an investment are exactly what make it useful as a payment rail:

**It settles in minutes, not days.** No correspondent banks, no cut-off times, no weekend freeze. A payment started at 11pm on a Saturday moves like one started at 11am on a Tuesday.

**It's cheap to move.** With no chain of intermediaries, the fees and the FX spread that eat into cross-border payments largely disappear.

**It's programmable.** Because it settles on open infrastructure, the payment logic can live in code — automated payouts, split settlements, and reconciliation that happen without a human touching them.

## The treasury lens

Think about what those three properties do to a finance operation:

- **Float shrinks.** Money in transit for three days becomes money available in minutes.
- **FX cost falls.** Conversion happens at the actual rate, not a bank's retail margin.
- **Reconciliation simplifies.** Every settlement is on-ledger and auditable by default.

For a business that pays suppliers or staff across several currencies, this isn't a marginal improvement. It changes how much working capital you need to hold and how much margin you give away on every payment.

## What a careful team should verify

Settlement rails are only as good as their controls. Before moving money this way, a treasury team should ask four questions:

1. **Who holds the keys?** A non-custodial model means you retain control of your assets rather than handing them to a third party.
2. **What's the regulatory standing?** You want a partner operating as a regulated Virtual Asset Service Provider, not an unlicensed desk.
3. **What does the last mile look like?** Settlement speed only matters if value lands in a real bank account — check the local clearing rails.
4. **How is conversion handled?** Conversion should be transparent and settlement automatic, not an opaque manual process.

On all four, the answer should be boring and specific. If it's vague, keep looking.

## The takeaway

Stablecoins are not a bet on crypto. They're a faster, cheaper settlement layer for money that already needs to move across borders. The only real decision is whether you move it through a correspondent chain — or through rails that settle in minutes.
